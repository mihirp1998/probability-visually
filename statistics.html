📈 Statistics

Probability goes from a known model to data: "if the coin is fair, how many heads?" Statistics goes the other way: "I saw 7 heads in 10 flips, what is the coin?" Two ideas cover most interview questions: maximum likelihood (how to pick an estimate) and bias & variance (how to judge it).

Maximum likelihood estimation

We observe data $x_1,\dots,x_n$, drawn i.i.d. from a distribution with an unknown parameter $\theta$. Maximum likelihood says: pick the $\theta$ under which the data we actually saw would have been most probable.

$$L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i;\theta),\qquad \hat\theta_{\text{MLE}}=\arg\max_\theta L(\theta).$$

Think of $\theta$ as a dial. For each dial setting, ask "how surprised would I be by this data?" The MLE is the setting that makes the data least surprising. Note that $L(\theta)$ is a function of $\theta$ with the data held fixed; it is not a probability distribution over $\theta$ (it need not integrate to 1).

The log-likelihood trick

Products are awkward to differentiate, and multiplying hundreds of small probabilities underflows to 0 on a computer. Since $\log$ is increasing, maximizing $\log L$ gives the same $\hat\theta$, and it turns the product into a sum:

$$\ell(\theta)=\log L(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\log p(x_i;\theta).$$

Recipe

  1. Write down $\ell(\theta)=\sum_i \log p(x_i;\theta)$ and simplify.
  2. Differentiate, set $\ell'(\theta)=0$, and solve for $\hat\theta$.
  3. Check it really is a maximum: $\ell''(\hat\theta)\lt 0$, and compare with the endpoints / boundaries of the allowed parameter range. Sometimes the maximum is on the boundary, where the derivative isn't zero at all (see the uniform example below).

The original said "check second derivative of endpoints". It should read: check the second derivative (to confirm a max, not a min) and check the endpoints / boundaries separately.

Example: Bernoulli (coin flips)

$x_i\in\{0,1\}$ i.i.d. $\Bern(p)$, with PMF $P(X=x;p)=p^x(1-p)^{1-x}$. Let $k=\sum_i x_i$ be the number of 1s.

$$\ell(p)=\sum_{i=1}^n\log\big[p^{x_i}(1-p)^{1-x_i}\big]=\sum_{i=1}^n\big[x_i\log p+(1-x_i)\log(1-p)\big]=k\log p+(n-k)\log(1-p).$$ $$\ell'(p)=\frac kp-\frac{n-k}{1-p}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat p=\frac kn.$$

The MLE is just the sample frequency, which is what you would have guessed.

The original wrote $\ell(\lambda)$ and $\ell'(\lambda)$ here. The parameter is $p$, so it is $\ell(p)$. Also, the per-sample term must use $x_i$ (not a bare $x$): $\sum_i x_i\log p+(1-x_i)\log(1-p)$.

Check it's a maximum

$\ell''(p)=-\dfrac{k}{p^2}-\dfrac{n-k}{(1-p)^2}\lt 0$ for all $p\in(0,1)$, so $\ell$ is concave and the critical point is the global max. Edge case: if $k=0$, then $\ell(p)=n\log(1-p)$ is decreasing and there is no interior critical point; the max is at the boundary $\hat p=0$, which still equals $k/n$.

Example: Exponential (waiting times)

$x_i$ i.i.d. $\Exp(\lambda)$ with density $f(x;\lambda)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$.

$$\ell(\lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^n\log\big(\lambda e^{-\lambda x_i}\big)=\sum_{i=1}^n\big(\log\lambda-\lambda x_i\big)=n\log\lambda-\lambda\sum_i x_i.$$ $$\ell'(\lambda)=\frac n\lambda-\sum_i x_i=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat\lambda_{\text{MLE}}=\frac{n}{\sum_i x_i}=\frac1{\bar x}.$$

The MLE is the reciprocal of the sample mean. That makes sense because $\Exp(\lambda)$ has mean $1/\lambda$: if buses arrive every 10 minutes on average, the rate is 0.1 per minute. ($\ell''=-n/\lambda^2\lt 0$, so it's a max.)

Example: Uniform(0, θ), where calculus fails

$x_i$ i.i.d. $\Unif(0,\theta)$ with density $1/\theta$ on $[0,\theta]$. If $\theta$ is smaller than any data point, that point would have probability 0, so

$$L(\theta)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{\theta^n} & \text{if } \theta\ge\max_i x_i\\[4pt] 0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

$1/\theta^n$ is decreasing, so we want $\theta$ as small as allowed: $\hat\theta=\max_i x_i$. Setting the derivative to zero would get you nowhere ($-n\theta^{-n-1}\ne0$); the maximum is at the boundary of the feasible region. This is exactly why the recipe says to check boundaries.

But the MLE here is biased: the max of the sample is always below $\theta$, so it underestimates on average.

$$\E[\max_i x_i]=\frac{n}{n+1}\,\theta \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{n+1}{n}\max_i x_i \text{ is unbiased.}$$
Proof of $\E[\max]=\frac{n}{n+1}\theta$

$P(\max\le x)=P(\text{all }x_i\le x)=(x/\theta)^n$, so the density is $n x^{n-1}/\theta^n$ on $[0,\theta]$ and

$$\E[\max]=\int_0^\theta x\cdot\frac{n x^{n-1}}{\theta^n}\,dx=\frac{n}{\theta^n}\cdot\frac{\theta^{n+1}}{n+1}=\frac{n}{n+1}\theta.$$

Shortcut: $n$ uniform points cut $[0,\theta]$ into $n+1$ gaps with equal expected length $\theta/(n+1)$, and the max sits one gap below $\theta$ (see uniform).

The uniform likelihood is a cliff

MLE = minimizing cross-entropy loss

Neural nets and logistic regression are trained to minimize the cross-entropy (negative log-likelihood) loss:

$$\text{CE}(\theta)=-\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\log p_\theta(x_i)\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \text{maximize }\ \ell(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^N\log p_\theta(x_i).$$

Minimizing one is the same as maximizing the other (the $-\frac1N$ is just a negative constant). In words: models are trained to make the observed data as probable as possible. For classification the "data" are labels, $p_\theta(y_i\mid x_i)$. Similarly, minimizing squared error is MLE under Gaussian noise, since $-\log\N(y;\hat y,\sigma^2)=\frac{(y-\hat y)^2}{2\sigma^2}+\text{const}$.

MAP: MLE plus a prior

MLE uses only the data. If you have a prior belief $p(\theta)$, Bayes' rule gives the posterior $p(\theta\mid x)\propto p(x\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)$, and the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate maximizes it:

$$\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\arg\max_\theta\Big[\underbrace{\ell(\theta)}_{\text{data fit}}+\underbrace{\log p(\theta)}_{\text{prior = regularizer}}\Big].$$
  • Coin with a $\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)$ prior: $\hat p_{\text{MAP}}=\dfrac{k+\alpha-1}{n+\alpha+\beta-2}$. A $\text{Beta}(2,2)$ prior acts like one extra head and one extra tail ("add-one smoothing").
  • Gaussian prior on weights → $\log p(w)=-\frac{\|w\|^2}{2\tau^2}+\text{const}$ → L2 regularization (weight decay). A Laplace prior gives L1.
  • A flat prior gives MAP = MLE. As $n\to\infty$, the data term dominates and MAP → MLE.
You flip a coin 10 times and get 0 heads. What are the MLE and the MAP estimate with a $\text{Beta}(2,2)$ prior?
  • MLE $=0.5$, MAP $=0$
  • MLE $=0$, MAP $=0$
  • MLE $=0$, MAP $=1/12$
  • MLE $=1/12$, MAP $=0$
MLE $=k/n=0$, which claims heads is impossible after only 10 flips. MAP $=(0+2-1)/(10+2+2-2)=1/12$: the prior keeps the estimate away from the extreme.

Bias & variance

An estimator $\hat\theta$ is a function of the data. The data are random, so $\hat\theta$ is a random variable: rerun the experiment and you get a different estimate. We judge an estimator by the distribution of its estimates around the true $\theta$.

Bias: off-center on average?
$\operatorname{Bias}(\hat\theta)=\E[\hat\theta]-\theta$
Variance: how much it jumps around
$\Var(\hat\theta)=\E\big[(\hat\theta-\E[\hat\theta])^2\big]$
MSE: total error
$\operatorname{MSE}(\hat\theta)=\E\big[(\hat\theta-\theta)^2\big]$
The dartboard picture
Each dart is the estimate from one fresh dataset; the bullseye is the true $\theta$. Bias is how far the center of the darts is from the bullseye (the cross marks the center). Variance is how spread out the darts are.
$$\operatorname{MSE}(\hat\theta)=\operatorname{Bias}(\hat\theta)^2+\Var(\hat\theta)$$
Proof of the decomposition

Add and subtract $\E[\hat\theta]$ inside the square:

$$\begin{aligned} \E[(\hat\theta-\theta)^2]&=\E\Big[\big((\hat\theta-\E\hat\theta)+(\E\hat\theta-\theta)\big)^2\Big]\\ &=\underbrace{\E[(\hat\theta-\E\hat\theta)^2]}_{\Var(\hat\theta)}+2(\E\hat\theta-\theta)\underbrace{\E[\hat\theta-\E\hat\theta]}_{=0}+\underbrace{(\E\hat\theta-\theta)^2}_{\operatorname{Bias}^2}. \end{aligned}$$

The cross term vanishes because $\E\hat\theta-\theta$ is a constant and $\hat\theta$ minus its own mean has mean zero. It's the same identity as $\E[X^2]=\Var(X)+\E[X]^2$, applied to $X=\hat\theta-\theta$.

Unbiased is not automatically best. A slightly biased estimator with much less variance can have smaller total error. That is the bias–variance trade-off behind regularization, shrinkage, and simpler models in ML.

Worked example: three ways to estimate θ in Uniform(0, θ)

EstimatorBiasVarianceMSE
$\max_i x_i$ (MLE)$-\dfrac{\theta}{n+1}$$\dfrac{n\theta^2}{(n+1)^2(n+2)}$$\dfrac{2\theta^2}{(n+1)(n+2)}$
$\frac{n+1}{n}\max_i x_i$$0$$\dfrac{\theta^2}{n(n+2)}$$\dfrac{\theta^2}{n(n+2)}$
$2\bar x$ (method of moments)$0$$\dfrac{\theta^2}{3n}$$\dfrac{\theta^2}{3n}$

Both corrected estimators are unbiased, but the max-based one has variance shrinking like $1/n^2$, versus $1/n$ for $2\bar x$. The max uses the most informative statistic. (Fun fact: the MSE-optimal multiple of the max is $\frac{n+2}{n+1}\max$, which is slightly biased.)

Where the variances come from

$\E[\max^2]=\int_0^\theta x^2\,\frac{nx^{n-1}}{\theta^n}dx=\frac{n}{n+2}\theta^2$, so $\Var(\max)=\frac{n}{n+2}\theta^2-\frac{n^2}{(n+1)^2}\theta^2=\frac{n\theta^2}{(n+1)^2(n+2)}$. Scaling by $\frac{n+1}{n}$ multiplies the variance by $\frac{(n+1)^2}{n^2}$.

For the mean: $\Var(x_i)=\theta^2/12$, so $\Var(2\bar x)=4\cdot\frac{\theta^2}{12n}=\frac{\theta^2}{3n}$.

Estimator shoot-out
We draw 4,000 datasets of size $n$ from $\Unif(0,\theta=1)$ and compute each estimator on each dataset. The histograms show the spread of estimates; the dashed line is the truth $\theta=1$ and the solid line is each estimator's average. The table checks $\text{MSE}=\text{Bias}^2+\text{Var}$.

The classic: sample variance. For Gaussian data the MLE of $\sigma^2$ is $\frac1n\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2$, which is biased: its mean is $\frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$. Using $\bar x$ instead of the true mean makes the deviations look a little too small. Dividing by $n-1$ (Bessel's correction) fixes the bias. Interviewers love asking "why $n-1$?"

With $n=10$ samples from $\Unif(0,\theta)$, which estimator has the smallest MSE?
  • $\frac{11}{10}\max_i x_i$
  • $\max_i x_i$
  • $2\bar x$
  • They're all equal since two are unbiased
MSEs: $\frac{11}{10}\max$: $\theta^2/120$. MLE $\max$: $2\theta^2/132=\theta^2/66$. $2\bar x$: $\theta^2/30$. Unbiasedness says nothing about variance, and $2\bar x$ is far noisier.
