🧩 Core problem patterns

Most interview probability questions are one of a handful of patterns in disguise. Three of them do most of the work: first-step analysis (when the process can restart), counting with indicators (when you need "how many, on average"), and max/min through the CDF (when you need an extreme value). Recognize the pattern and the algebra is usually short.

If the question sounds like…reach for…
"expected number of flips/rolls/steps until …"first-step analysis: set up states and condition on the next step
"expected number of things that …" (fixed points, empty boxes, runs, pairs)indicators + linearity
"expected largest / smallest / first to …"CDF of the max, tail of the min

First-step analysis

After one step, you are usually in a slightly different copy of the same problem. Write the answer from every possible "where am I now?" state in terms of the answers from the states you could move to next. That gives a system of linear equations, and you solve it.

Classic question: what is the expected number of fair coin flips needed to see two heads in a row (HH)?

The only thing that matters about the past is how much progress you have toward HH. Define:

  • $E_0$ = expected remaining flips when you have no progress (you just started, or just flipped tails);
  • $E_1$ = expected remaining flips when the last flip was a head (one H in hand).

Now condition on the next flip. It always costs 1 flip, and then you land in a new state:

$$E_0=\underbrace{\tfrac12 (1+E_0)}_{\text{tails: no progress}}+\underbrace{\tfrac12(1+E_1)}_{\text{heads: one H}} \qquad\qquad E_1=\underbrace{\tfrac12(1+E_0)}_{\text{tails: start over}}+\underbrace{\tfrac12\cdot 1}_{\text{heads: done}}$$
Solving the system

Simplify the second equation: $E_1 = 1+\tfrac12E_0$. Substitute into the first:

$$E_0 = 1+\tfrac12E_0+\tfrac12\big(1+\tfrac12E_0\big) = \tfrac32+\tfrac34E_0 \;\Rightarrow\; \tfrac14E_0=\tfrac32\;\Rightarrow\; E_0=6,\quad E_1=4.$$

The original notes wrote the "heads again" branch of $E_1$ as $\tfrac12\cdot 0$. The flip that finishes still costs one, so it should be $\tfrac12\cdot 1$ (equivalently $\tfrac12(1+0)$). With $\cdot\,0$ the equations would give $E_0=5$, not 6. The stated answer $E_0=6$ is right.

First-step analysis recipe.

  1. Choose states that capture everything about the past that matters for the future (here: the longest useful prefix of the target).
  2. For each state $s$, write $E_s = 1 + \sum_{s'} P(s\to s')\,E_{s'}$, with $E=0$ in the finishing state.
  3. Solve the linear system. The answer is $E_{\text{start}}$.

The same move gives probabilities instead of expectations: $p_s=\sum_{s'}P(s\to s')\,p_{s'}$, with $p=1$ in winning states and $p=0$ in losing ones. That is how you solve gambler's ruin.

Don't always reset to the start on a miss. In HTH, if you have "H" and flip H, you are still at "H" because the new head is a fresh start of the pattern. Likewise, in HHT, a third H keeps you at "HH". The right state after a miss is the longest suffix of what you have seen that is still a prefix of the target. This is the same idea as the KMP string-matching automaton.

Shortcut for fair coins (the "overlap" or ABRACADABRA formula). For a pattern of length $L$, add $2^k$ for every $k$ such that the first $k$ letters equal the last $k$ letters:

$$\E[\text{flips}] = \sum_{k\,:\,\text{prefix}_k=\text{suffix}_k} 2^k.$$

HH: $2+4=6$. HT: $4$ (no proper overlap). HHH: $2+4+8=14$. HTH: $2+8=10$. For a biased coin, replace $2^k$ with $1/P(\text{prefix}_k)$. It is a lovely way to check an answer, but in an interview, set up the states first.

HT and HH both have probability $\tfrac14$ at any given pair of positions. Why does HT take fewer flips on average (4 vs 6)?
  • Because tails are more likely than heads after a head.
  • Because when you hold an H and miss, HT leaves you at "H" (the miss was an H), while HH sends you back to the start (the miss was a T).
  • Because HT can overlap with itself and HH cannot.
  • It doesn't. They both take 6 on average.
From state "H": aiming for HT, a miss means you flipped H, so you are still at "H" and lose no progress. Aiming for HH, a miss means you flipped T, which wipes out your progress. HH does overlap with itself, and self-overlap is exactly what makes a pattern slower (see the overlap formula).

The same trick, once more: the mean of a geometric variable. Each trial succeeds with probability $p$. Otherwise you've spent one trial and are back at the start: $E=1+(1-p)E\Rightarrow E=1/p$.

This generalizes. The states and arrows above form an absorbing Markov chain, and first-step analysis is just the general equation for its expected hitting times, $h_s = 1+\sum_{s'}P_{ss'}h_{s'}$. See Markov chains for the matrix version, and gambler's ruin and random walks for more examples.

Counting in expectation

"How many, on average?" is hard to answer by finding the whole distribution of the count, and easy to answer by splitting the count into one yes/no question per item. The expected count is the sum of the probabilities, and this holds even if the items are wildly dependent.

  1. Define an indicator for each item: $X_i=\1[\text{item } i \text{ has the property}]$.
  2. The count is $N=\sum_i X_i$.
  3. Compute $\E[X_i]=P(\text{item } i \text{ has the property})$. Usually symmetry makes this the same for every $i$.
  4. Linearity of expectation: $\E[N]=\sum_i P(X_i=1)$.

Fixed points of a permutation

Shuffle $n$ cards labeled $1,\dots,n$ into positions $1,\dots,n$. A fixed point is a card that lands in its own position. (The story version: $n$ people throw their hats in a pile and each grabs one at random. How many get their own hat?)

Let $X_i=\1[\text{card } i \text{ lands in position } i]$. By symmetry, position $i$ is equally likely to hold any of the $n$ cards, so $P(X_i=1)=\tfrac1n$. Therefore

$$\E[\#\text{fixed points}] = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac1n = 1 \qquad\text{for every } n.$$
Shuffle and count fixed points
Each box shows the card that landed in that slot. Green boxes are fixed points (card $i$ in slot $i$). Shuffle many times and the histogram of the number of fixed points approaches the exact distribution (dots), which is almost exactly $\Pois(1)$ (rings) for any $n\gtrsim 5$.
Bonus: the variance is also 1 (second-moment trick)

The $X_i$ are dependent, but we only need pairwise probabilities. For $i\neq j$, $P(X_i=X_j=1)=\frac1n\cdot\frac1{n-1}$. Using $X_i^2=X_i$:

$$\E[N^2]=\sum_i\E[X_i]+\sum_{i\neq j}\E[X_iX_j]=1+n(n-1)\cdot\frac{1}{n(n-1)}=2 \;\Rightarrow\;\Var(N)=2-1^2=1.$$

Mean 1 and variance 1 match $\Pois(1)$. The probability of no fixed points tends to $e^{-1}\approx0.368$, which is the derangement result.

Linearity needs no independence. Variance does. $\E[\sum X_i]=\sum\E[X_i]$ always holds. But $\Var(\sum X_i)=\sum\Var(X_i)$ only holds when the covariances vanish. For dependent indicators, use the second-moment trick above.

More indicator examples

Expected number of HH occurrences (overlapping) in $n$ fair flips

Let $X_i=\1[\text{flips } i \text{ and } i+1 \text{ are both H}]$ for $i=1,\dots,n-1$. Each has probability $\tfrac14$, so $\E=\frac{n-1}{4}$. The $X_i$ overlap and are dependent, and that doesn't matter.

Expected number of empty bins when throwing $m$ balls into $m$ bins

$X_j=\1[\text{bin } j \text{ empty}]$, and $P(X_j=1)=(1-\tfrac1m)^m\to e^{-1}$. So $\E[\#\text{empty}]=m(1-\tfrac1m)^m\approx 0.37m$. This is the "37% empty" fact from balls into bins.

Expected number of distinct values in $k$ rolls of an $n$-sided die

Use an indicator per face: $X_v=\1[\text{face } v \text{ appears at least once}]$, with $P=1-(1-\tfrac1n)^k$. So $\E=n\big(1-(1-\tfrac1n)^k\big)$. Tip: when indicators over the obvious items are awkward, try indicators over the other side (faces instead of rolls).

Expected number of records (left-to-right maxima) in a random permutation

Position $i$ is a record if it is the largest of the first $i$ values. By symmetry this has probability $\tfrac1i$, so $\E=\sum_{i=1}^n\tfrac1i=H_n\approx\ln n$.

A random permutation of $1..n$. What is the expected number of $i$ such that $i$ and $i+1$ are adjacent in that order (i.e. $i$ immediately followed by $i+1$)?
  • $\tfrac1n$
  • $\tfrac{n-1}{2}$
  • $1-\tfrac1n$
  • $1$
There are $n-1$ indicators, one per pair $(i,i+1)$. Glue $i,i+1$ into a block: there are $(n-1)!$ such permutations out of $n!$, so each has probability $\tfrac1n$. Total: $\tfrac{n-1}{n}=1-\tfrac1n$.

Max/min of $n$ RVs

The maximum is at most $x$ exactly when every $X_i$ is at most $x$. The minimum is above $x$ exactly when every $X_i$ is above $x$. "Every" turns into a product when the $X_i$ are independent. So for the max, work with the CDF, and for the min, work with the tail.

Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be iid with CDF $F_X$.

Max $M=\max_iX_i$

$$F_M(x)=P(M\le x)=P(\text{all }X_i\le x)=[F_X(x)]^n$$

Min $m=\min_iX_i$

$$P(m>x)=P(\text{every }X_i>x)=[1-F_X(x)]^n$$ $$F_m(x)=1-[1-F_X(x)]^n$$

The original wrote $P(\text{every }X_i>m)$. It should be $>x$. Also, in the uniform example, $F_m(x)=1-P(m\ge x)$ should strictly be $1-P(m>x)$. The two are equal for continuous variables, but not in general.

Two ways to get the expectation:

  1. 3-step recipe: CDF → differentiate to get the PDF → integrate $\int x f(x)\,dx$.
  2. Tail formula (non-negative variables only, and usually faster): $\E[X]=\int_0^\infty P(X>x)\,dx$.
Why the tail formula works

Write $X=\int_0^\infty \1[x\lt X]\,dx$ (the length of $[0,X)$). Take expectations and swap $\E$ and $\int$:

$$\E[X]=\int_0^\infty \E\,\1[X>x]\,dx=\int_0^\infty P(X>x)\,dx.$$

The discrete version, for $X\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}$, is $\E[X]=\sum_{k\ge 0}P(X>k)$.

Uniform$[0,1]$ example

Result: $\E[\max]=\frac{n}{n+1}$ and $\E[\min]=\frac{1}{n+1}$.

Max

  • CDF: $F_M(x)=\prod_i P(X_i\le x)=x^n$
  • PDF: $f_M(x)=nx^{n-1}$
  • $\E[M]=\int_0^1 x\cdot nx^{n-1}\,dx=\frac{n}{n+1}$
  • Tail: $\int_0^1(1-x^n)\,dx=1-\frac1{n+1}=\frac{n}{n+1}$

Min

  • Tail: $P(m>x)=(1-x)^n$
  • CDF: $F_m(x)=1-(1-x)^n$
  • PDF: $f_m(x)=n(1-x)^{n-1}$
  • $\E[m]=\int_0^1(1-x)^n\,dx=\frac{1}{n+1}$
Integral for $\E[m]$ via the PDF route

Substitute $u=1-x$: $\int_0^1 x\,n(1-x)^{n-1}dx=n\int_0^1(1-u)u^{n-1}du=n\Big(\frac1n-\frac1{n+1}\Big)=\frac1{n+1}$. The tail formula gets there in one line, which is why it is worth remembering.

The gap picture. $n$ random points cut $[0,1]$ into $n+1$ gaps. By symmetry (imagine the points on a circle of length 1 with one extra "cut" at 0), all $n+1$ gaps have the same distribution, so each has mean $\frac{1}{n+1}$. The min is the first gap: $\frac1{n+1}$. The max is $1$ minus the last gap: $\frac{n}{n+1}$. More generally, the $k$-th smallest point has mean $\frac{k}{n+1}$.

  • $\tfrac14$
  • $\tfrac13$
  • $\tfrac34$

$\E[\max]-\E[\min]=\tfrac34-\tfrac14=\tfrac12$. With the gap picture: 4 gaps of mean $\tfrac14$, and the range spans the 2 middle ones. In general the range has mean $\frac{n-1}{n+1}$.

Exponential example

For $X_i\sim\Exp(\lambda)$ iid:

$$P(m>x)=[P(X_i>x)]^n=e^{-n\lambda x}\;\Rightarrow\; m\sim\Exp(n\lambda),\qquad \E[m]=\int_0^\infty e^{-n\lambda x}\,dx=\frac{1}{n\lambda}.$$

This holds with different rates too: $\min_i X_i\sim\Exp(\lambda_1+\dots+\lambda_n)$, and $X_j$ is the smallest with probability $\lambda_j/\sum_i\lambda_i$ (see exponential).

Race of exponential clocks
Three independent clocks ring at $\Exp(\lambda_1)$, $\Exp(\lambda_2)$, $\Exp(\lambda_3)$ times. The histogram shows the time of the first ring, and the curve is the $\Exp(\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\lambda_3)$ density. The readout shows how often each clock wins against $\lambda_j/\sum\lambda$.

The original notes say the max is "a lot more complicated" but grows logarithmically. There is actually a slick derivation using memorylessness:

$\E[\max]=\frac1\lambda\left(1+\frac12+\dots+\frac1n\right)=\frac{H_n}{\lambda}\approx\frac{\ln n+0.577}{\lambda}$

Think of $n$ light bulbs. The first failure happens after $\Exp(n\lambda)$ time, with mean $\frac1{n\lambda}$. By memorylessness the $n-1$ survivors are "as good as new", so the next failure takes another $\Exp((n-1)\lambda)$, and so on. The max is the time of the last failure:

$$\E[M]=\frac1{n\lambda}+\frac1{(n-1)\lambda}+\dots+\frac1\lambda=\frac{H_n}{\lambda}.$$

Gaussian example

Result: $\E[\max_i X_i]\approx\sqrt{2\ln n}$.

For $X_i\sim\N(0,1)$ iid, the max grows, but extremely slowly. Even with $n=1000$ samples the expected max is only about $3.24$.

Back-of-envelope derivation

The max sits around the level $x$ that only about one of the $n$ samples exceeds: $n\,P(X>x)\approx1$. Gaussian tails are roughly $P(X>x)\approx e^{-x^2/2}$ (ignoring polynomial factors), so $n e^{-x^2/2}\approx1\Rightarrow x\approx\sqrt{2\ln n}$. The ignored factors make this an overestimate at practical $n$: for $n=100$ the exact value is about $2.51$, while $\sqrt{2\ln 100}\approx3.03$. The ratio does tend to 1, very slowly, so $\sqrt{2\ln n}$ is the right order of growth and the standard answer to quote.

Interview reflexes for extremes. For the max, use the CDF to the $n$-th power. For the min, use the tail to the $n$-th power. For non-negative variables, integrate the tail. For uniforms, use the gap picture. For exponentials, use memorylessness. For Gaussians, quote $\sqrt{2\ln n}$ for the order of growth.
